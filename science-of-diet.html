The Science of Our Diet

****This blog will only repeat scientific information that has been cited by creditable institutions.****

Never in the history of civilisation are we so conscious of not only the importance of a good diet but also the detrimental effects of a poor diet. The science of food has advanced past just the understanding of the nutrients that can be attained from foods but we have a clearer picture of what food compositions have on the body both physically and psychologically.

However, with information so accessible and advice been easily posted, there is significant information which is very much in the pseudoscience bucket i.e. has not been scientifically validated. This leads to misinformation which subsequently contradicts what is and isn’t good for someone’s WELL-BEING thus causing further mistrust of the information that has been scientifically validated.

One of the understandings of our diet is how it is impacting our gut[1]. These bacteria produce compounds; short chain fatty acids (SCFA), which are vital for gut health, strengthening the intestinal barrier, and reducing inflammation.

From a dietary perspective, an unhealthy gut arises primarily from processed and high fat foods. To counter this one of the essential SCFA is Butyrate, which is produced by gut bacteria fermenting dietary fibre in the colon. The benefits of Butyrate are that it possesses anti-inflammatory properties, helps regulate the immune system, and may support brain health. Where a gut is inflamed leads to a “leaky gut” this inflammation breaks down the tight junctions holding intestinal cells together, allowing toxins and bacteria to leak into the bloodstream. The impact on the body is cramps, food sensitivities, fatigue, headaches, and joint pain, to name a few[2].

Lack of butyrate signalling results in nitrate electron acceptors being released into the colon, which facultative anaerobes can also use for cell respiration, breaking down carbohydrates into carbon dioxide rather than fermenting them[3]. If this is occurring it generally indicates a shift toward a less efficient, potentially inflammatory state (gut dysbiosis).

Which brings us to the Gut-Brain Axis. This is where the gut and the brain are in constant communication with one another via the vagus nerve. This connection influences digestion, mood, immunity, and stress responses. The gut is covered in nerves lining the digestive tract which is named the enteric nervous system (ENS).

When the Gut-Brain Axis is in-sync we are more balanced and are in a higher state allowing us to take advantage of the many positives which come from being clear in body and mind.
