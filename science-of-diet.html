The Science of Our Diet

****This blog will only repeat scientific information that has been cited by creditable institutions.****

Never in the history of civilisation are we so conscious of not only the importance of a good diet but also the detrimental effects of a poor diet 1. The science of food has advanced past just the understanding of the nutrients that can be attained from foods but we have a clearer picture of what food compositions have on the body both physically and psychologically.

However, with information so accessible and advice been easily posted, there is significant information which is very much in the pseudoscience bucket i.e. has not been scientifically validated. This leads to misinformation which subsequently contradicts what is and isn’t good for someone’s WELL-BEING 2 thus causing further mistrust of the information that has been scientifically validated.

One of the understandings of our diet is how it is impacting our gut 3. When we hear the word gut, we think of not only the digestive area, stomach, small and large intestines but also a place where we feel our emotions. Research is gaining a deeper understanding of how our gut impacts us. From the perspective of food, the gut refers specifically to the digestive area. This is where the food is further broken down and where nutrients from the food types are absorbed into the blood.

What is not absorbed enters the large intestine where microbes digest on the fibre and also absorbs water. It is understood that there are trillions of microbes in the large intestine breaking the food down. This is known as the gut microbiota environment and it is important that the composition is healthy as it allows the food not absorbed to leave the body easily.

These bacteria produce compounds; short chain fatty acids (SCFA), which are vital for gut health, strengthening the intestinal barrier, and reducing inflammation, which in turn aids overall immune regulation. Where a gut is inflamed leads to a “leaky gut” this inflammation breaks down the tight junctions holding intestinal cells together, allowing toxins and bacteria to leak into the bloodstream. The impact on the body is cramps, food sensitivities, fatigue, headaches, and joint pain, to name a few. It is also been linked with chronic fatigue syndrome, which manifests itself brain fog, and sleep disruption.

There is still significant research required to determine the true impact of gut health on our psychological state, but the evidence is pointing to something more profound regarding our holistic health. From a dietary perspective, an unhealthy gut arises primarily from processed and high fat foods. To counter this one of the essential SCFA is Butyrate, which is produced by gut bacteria fermenting dietary fibre in the colon. Butyrate is produced internally by consuming fibre-rich foods (whole grains, legumes, fruits, vegetables, nuts). The benefits of Butyrate are that it possesses anti-inflammatory properties, helps regulate the immune system, and may support brain health.

Lack of butyrate signalling results in nitrate electron acceptors being released into the colon, which facultative anaerobes can also use for cell respiration, breaking down carbohydrates into carbon dioxide rather than fermenting them. If this is occurring it generally indicates a shift toward a less efficient, potentially inflammatory state (gut dysbiosis). Which brings us to the GUT-BRAIN AXIS. This is where the gut and the brain are in constant communication with one another via the vagus nerve. This connection influences digestion, mood, immunity, and stress responses. The gut is full of tiny microbes which is collectively called the microbiome that produce chemicals that help shape brain functions and mood e.g. serotonin which regulates mood, sleep, appetite, and digestion. The gut is covered in nerves lining the digestive tract which is named the enteric nervous system (ENS). This functions by sending signals to the brain. The axis is involved in pain sensitivity, stress, anxiety, cognitive function, and metabolic health. A healthy microbiome—supported by fermented foods, prebiotics, and fibre—is crucial for maintaining this axis.

What we have briefly, discussed is demonstrating the importance our diet has on both on our physiology and psychology. When the gut-brain axis is in-sync we are more balanced and are in a higher state allowing us to take advantage of the many positives which come from being clear in body and mind.
