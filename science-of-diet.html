The Science of Our Diet

****This blog will only repeat scientific information that has been cited by creditable institutions[cite: 93].****

Never in the history of civilisation are we so conscious of not only the importance of a good diet but also the detrimental effects of a poor diet[cite: 94]. The science of food has advanced past just the understanding of the nutrients that can be attained from foods but we have a clearer picture of what food compositions have on the body both physically and psychologically[cite: 95]. However, with information so accessible and advice been easily posted, there is significant information which is very much in the pseudoscience bucket i.e. has not been scientifically validated[cite: 96]. This leads to misinformation which subsequently contradicts what is and isn’t good for someone’s WELL-BEING thus causing further mistrust of information that has been scientifically validated[cite: 97].

One of the understandings of our diet is how it is impacting our gut[cite: 98]. When we hear the word gut, we think of not only the digestive area, stomach, small and large intestines but also a place where we feel our emotions[cite: 99]. Research is gaining a deeper understanding of how our gut impacts us[cite: 100]. From the perspective of food, the gut refers specifically to the digestive area[cite: 101]. This is where the food is further broken down and where nutrients from the food types are absorbed into the blood[cite: 102]. What is not absorbed enters the large intestine where microbes digest on the fibre and also absorbs water[cite: 103]. It is understood that there are trillions of microbes in the large intestine breaking the food down[cite: 104]. This is known as the gut microbiota environment and it is important that the composition is healthy as it allows the food not absorbed to leave the body easily[cite: 105].

These bacteria produce compounds; short chain fatty acids (SCFA), which are vital for gut health, strengthening the intestinal barrier, and reducing inflammation, which in turn aids overall immune regulation[cite: 106]. Where a gut is inflamed leads to a “leaky gut” this inflammation breaks down the tight junctions holding intestinal cells together, allowing toxins and bacteria to leak into the bloodstream[cite: 107]. The impact on the body is cramps, food sensitivities, fatigue, headaches, and joint pain[cite: 108]. It is also been linked with chronic fatigue syndrome, which manifests itself brain fog, and sleep disruption[cite: 109].

To counter this one of the essential SCFA is Butyrate, which is produced by gut bacteria fermenting dietary fibre in the colon[cite: 112]. Butyrate is produced internally by consuming fibre-rich foods (whole grains, legumes, fruits, vegetables, nuts)[cite: 113]. The benefits are that it possesses anti-inflammatory properties, helps regulate the immune system, and may support brain health[cite: 114]. Lack of butyrate signalling results in nitrate electron acceptors being released into the colon, which facultative anaerobes can also use for cell respiration, breaking down carbohydrates into carbon dioxide rather than fermenting them[cite: 115]. This generally indicates a shift toward a less efficient, potentially inflammatory state known as gut dysbiosis[cite: 116].

Which brings us to the GUT-BRAIN AXIS. This is where the gut and the brain are in constant communication via the vagus nerve[cite: 117]. This connection influences digestion, mood, immunity, and stress responses[cite: 118]. The microbiome produces chemicals like serotonin which regulates mood, sleep, and appetite[cite: 118, 119]. The enteric nervous system (ENS) sends signals to the brain, involving pain sensitivity, stress, and anxiety[cite: 119, 120]. A healthy microbiome—supported by fermented foods, prebiotics, and fibre—is crucial for maintaining this axis[cite: 121]. When the gut-brain axis is in-sync we are more balanced and clear in body and mind[cite: 123].
