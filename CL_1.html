Conscientiousness

The Conscientious Life (Part 1)

What does it mean to be Conscientious? The Oxford Reference Dictionary defines it: "showing or done with careful attention"[cite: 1, 2]. At its basis, it is the act of being careful before acting[cite: 3].

Conscientiousness is one of the "Big Five" personality traits[cite: 4]. The opposite would be behaving rashly, with no consideration for how one behaves or the consequences of such behavior[cite: 4]. While the modern world often aligns this trait with one's work—conjuring notions of diligence, persistence, and efficiency—it is much more than that[cite: 5, 6, 7]. It refers to completing obligations to others in a serious manner[cite: 7].

"There is an element of duty, and that is one of the defining aspects of the Conscientious Life." [cite: 9]

Our freedom is predicated on the fact that we have reason[cite: 10]. This allows us to consciously weigh a scenario and act accordingly, rather than following mere instinct[cite: 10]. However, in our post-modern world, individual desires are placed as paramount[cite: 11]. This "never-ending striving" has led to environmental degradation and unsteady societal structures, such as the erosion of truth[cite: 11].

If we take the opposite trait of conscientiousness—disorder—we see a life that is not in sync with itself or its environment, leading to chaos both within and without[cite: 12, 13]. To be truly conscientious, one’s consciousness must be elevated to grasp both the internal and the external world[cite: 14]. Without this awareness, we feel adrift, living as isolated "atoms" rather than parts of a society[cite: 15].

We find ourselves always moving toward the future with goals and plans which place a "veil" upon us, distracting us from the fundamental truths of the NOW[cite: 16, 17, 18, 19]. The Conscientious Life is not something we adhere to now and then; it is a mode of being that attempts to unite these strands together[cite: 20].

Immanual Kant spoke of a "Kingdom of Ends," implying that one should act toward others and the environment as ends in themselves, not just as a means to a subjective end[cite: 26]. Yet, the historical dialect has led us to the "final end" of humanity as isolated individuals where self-interest is paramount[cite: 29]. This aligns with attaining as much as possible with little regard for everything else[cite: 30].

This mode of understanding is no longer justified[cite: 33]. We must choose: either remain oblivious while the world as we know it ceases to be, or become active citizens who deliberately make decisions where actions are not just for the "I"[cite: 34].
